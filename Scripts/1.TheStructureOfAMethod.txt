-- Intro sequence -- 

This is a method known as plain bob doubles, one of the first methods you learn. This view here is the most common type of method visualisation. Each number represents that bell ringing, and the method can be read from left to right top to bottom. Certain numbers are replaced with lines to better visualise how that number moves, with each different colour representing a different bell. All methods start with rounds, so we can tell that the red line represents the treble and the blue line represents the 5.
A couple of important things to note are that the method starts and ends with rounds, there are no repeated rows, and between each row every bell can only move a maximum of one position. For example, from this row the 4 could go left, go right or stay in the same place, it could not move two positions. This also means that here, if the 4 goes left, the 3 must go right.

A method is split up into sections called "leads", with each lead being distinguished by a horizontal line known as a rule-off. The row below a rule off is known as the "lead head" and the row above is known as the "lead end". A change represents how the bells "change" between two rows, and so this change is known as the lead end change. Something very important to note is that the pattern that the bells make within each lead is identical. If we ignore the bell numbers and just use lines to show the path each bell takes and then overlay each lead you can see that they are identical. This sort of visualisation is known as the "grid" view, and it shows the exact same information as in the normal view, just in a more condensed form.

You can see from this "grid view" that for the majority of each lead the bells are following a simple pattern of hunting out to the back by moving to the right each time, lying by staying in that position for two rows, and then hunting in to the front to lead. If we continue this pattern you can see that we get back to rounds at the next row, however in plain bob we do something different and instead have one bell make seconds, two bells dodge and one bell make 4 blows at the back, so after our first lead our bells are in this order.

Lets explore what each of these terms mean, as these patterns are very common throughout all change ringing. Making seconds involves doing something called "Making a place", where your bell stays in the same position during a change. A place could be in any position and after a place you could go in either direction, in this case we come back to the front to lead again. Dodging involves your bell following this pattern, for example, here the 5 follows the pattern of 2 3 4 3 4 5ths place. We call this dodging up as the overall direction we are goinging is "up" to the back. In this context, up and out mean the same thing. Now because of how bells move, there must always be another bell dodging with us in the opposite direction whenever we do a dodge, and in this context it is the 2 dodging down. This is very useful when you are dodging as you can keep an eye on who you are dodging with. Finally, the 4 does 4 blows behind, where they stay at the back for 4 blows instead of 2.

Something to note is that its quite easy to work out what all the other bells are doing whilst doing your work at the lead end. For example, in this sitatuion say I was the 5. I can see that I am dodging with the 2, and when im not going over the 2 I am following the 3, so that bell must be making seconds. The 1 is always leading at a lead end and so by process of elimination the 4 must be at the back. This is useful when conducting to put people right. Note that this is harder for the bell making seconds as you have to keep track of which bells are ringing after you, but it is still definitely doable.

After the first lead we can see the bells are in this order, with this row being the lead head of the next lead. Putting the leads next to each other, notice how where the first lead started with rounds the second leads starts with this different row. Note that these two rows are the same row and are just laid out this way for convenience. Lets just focus on a single bell here *look at the 5*. In its first lead it follows this pattern, dodging 3/4 up. Now at this lead head it is in 3ths place. We know that in each lead the bells follow the same pattern, and so it must now follow the path that the 3 followed in the first lead! This means that after a bell follows the pattern 5 does in the first lead, it will follow the pattern that 3 does the next lead. This is more commonly phased that after being 5ths place bell, you will be 3rds place bell. Now continuing this pattern, after being 3rds place bell you will be 2nds place bell, then 4ths place bell, and finally back to 5ths place bell where we started, putting us back in rounds.
Since we follow this pattern, we do the work: 3/4 up, 2nds, 3/4 down, 4 blows behind in that order. If instead we started as bell 3, we still do this order, just offset by one: 2nds, 3/4 down, 4 blows and finally 3/4 up. This also means that if the conductor tells you that you are "now 4ths place bell", you know exactly where you are and what you are doing next (4 blows behind). In the normal view of a method place bells for the highlighted bell are often shown to the side of each lead head.

Lets look at a different way to visualise this known as the circle of work. This is which work each bell is doing at the first lead. I've put it in this order such that to see what work each bell is doing next we can just rotate the bells around. You can see that after 4 rotations we are back to where we started and so we get back to rounds. The number of rotations required to get back to rounds is where we get the number of leads from.

Looking further into how this rotation trick works, after the first lead we have shuffled the bells around in a very specific way to get the next lead head. Since we know that the pattern the bells take is the same between every lead we know that to get to the next lead head we can just apply the exact same shuffle. This forms a cycle of which place bell each bell is in, and since the length of the cycle is 4, after applying the permutation 4 times we get back to where we started, rounds!

Notice how there is one bell that we haven't mentioned yet, the treble stays in the same place after each lead and so no other bell can start doing its work and the treble can't start doing any other bells work. If a bell follows the same path each lead, it is known as a hunt bell and this is what makes the treble so simple to ring. If instead we had a method where there are no hunt bells, we would have a method with 5 leads and 5 different pieces of work to learn. This is because the circle of work would have 5 places instead of 4. Conversely, if we had two hunt bells we would only have 3 leads as there are only 3 bells which can rotate their work.

The upside of having lead ends work like this is that you can ring using place bells. If you know the work for each bell in each lead it's easy to start from a different bell and to deal with calls. You can envision that when you ring on a differnet bell you are just starting somewhere else along the method, going until you get back to where you started.
