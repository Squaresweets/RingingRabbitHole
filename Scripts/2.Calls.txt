A call occurs whenever the conductor shouts "Bob" or "Single" in order to change what each bell does next. A call can happen at the end of any lead, and typically modifies the lead end change. For example, in plain bob doubles if a bob was called here then instead of doing this lead end change you would do this change. This then modifies the lead head of the next lead and so changes what the bells are doing next.

So lets look at what you have to do when there is a call in plain bob doubles and why. If you are 5ths place bell you are normally about to dodge 3/4 up and become 3rds place bell. If a bob is called you instead make a place at the lead end change. This makes you 4ths place bell, and 4ths place bell starts by hunting in and then doing 4 blows behind. So essentially this is your line, you make 4ths, hunt back in and then do 4 blows behind next time. This pattern is known as "making the bob". You have, in effect, started doing what the 2 would have done if there was no call. For the rest of the line where you would have seen the 2 you will instead see the 5.
If you are 3rds place bell instead of making seconds you hunt out and become thirds place bell, and so make seconds next time. If you are 2nds place bell instead of dodging 3/4 down you hunt in and become 2nds place bell, thus it is as if you are finishing making seconds and so do 3/4 down next time. Finally if you are 4ths place bell the call does not effect you, and so since your place bell has not changed you just do what you would have done normally.
All these changes can be visualised in our circle of work with this transition instead of the normal rotation. Notice how the bell which is doing 4 blows behind still does its normal rotation.

In this visualisation I am only showing the lead head of each lead in a composition. A white arrow represents a plain lead, and a blue arrow represents a lead where a bob is called near the end. This cycle shows the lead heads we cycle through with just plain leads. If we add a bob and continue having plain leads you can see that we have entered a new cycle and are unable to get back to rounds without a new call. This new cycle is known as a different course. Each course can be thought of as the method but if it started with a different row instead of rounds. Each course has a course head, and for the normal course (known as the plain course) the "course head" is just rounds, however in this new course the course head is the lead head where our observation bell is in its natural position (where it was at the start). The observation bell is typically the bell that the conductor rings and its path is used to remember where to shout calls. It doesn't matter which lead we start a course from, it is still the same course.

This explanation of course heads gets a little tricky. For a lot of compositions the observation bell is left uneffected, meaning that the person ringing that bell never has to change what they are doing because of a call. The advantage of this is that the course head can just be thought of the equivalent of rounds for the new course, as the observation bell is always at its home position when we would have rounds. To illustrate what I mean, let's take the 3 as our observation bell. The only position where we can do a call and leave the 3 uneffected is here, as the 3 is making 4 blows behind. In this case we swap around the 2 4 and 5 like this. You can now imagine that 5 has taken over 4s role, 2 has taken over 4s role and 4 has taken over 2s role. This means that instead of getting back to rounds we get back to rounds but with these bells swapped around. Since our observation bell is at its natural position here, this is our new course head. You can imagine that to get to the lead head that we got to with the call, you could either have started from rounds and then had the call, or you could have started from this new course head and not have had a call.

The reason we have the course head defined like this instead of just being rounds with the effect of the calls, is that it allows us to uniquely define the course head of each course, otherwise we could have two courses which are identical but defined by different course heads. For example this could allow us to define a course with this row as the course head, despite this course being identical to the plain course when rotated round.

This all gets more complicated when the observation bell is effected by calls, especially when dealing with coursing order (comment: which will be covered in a future video) however the same concepts apply. You can work out what row rounds becomes in the new course by moving around the bells the call effects, however to find the new course head you need to go through the method until you find a lead end with the observation bell in its natural position. It isn't common to create a composition of plain bob doubles with the observation bell uneffected, as there is always only one position that you can do a call (when it is doing 4 blows behind).

Before going further I want to get you comfortable with the concepts of permutations as they can be a big shortcut when dealing with calls. I mentioned in the previous episode that a single lead shuffles around the bells in a very specific way and that since the path the bells take in each lead is the same you can just apply this shuffle again to get to the next lead head. This set of lines describing how to move the bells around is known as a permutation. If you have a set of changes, you can ignore the rows in between the start and the end and just use a permutation to tell you what the result of those changes are for any  initial row. You can also do this in reverse, working out what starting row to have to get the final row. If you know the permutation for a plain, bob and single lead you can easily check through a whole composition just by transposing the lead heads. This prevents you needing to write out all the rows inside the leads.

Now something to note is that the original course isn't the only one which contains rounds, and any row with the treble leading could be rounds if we pick the right course head. This row has the treble leading so lets try and find a course with that row as rounds. Lets find what rounds would have to be to make that row rounds. In the plain course this permutation is used (Animation of the lines moving to the other lines!) to get from the rounds to that row, and so we can apply this in reverse to rounds to find what that row would need to be. If we show the method starting from this row you can see that we get rounds where we expected. We can find the course head of this new course by looking for the row with our observation bell in its natural position.
Now you may think that doing this process on one of these other rows with the treble leading would give us a new course which contains rounds, however that does not work. It instead gives us the same course just with rounds being a different lead head of that course, and since a course is uniquely defined by the course head these are the same courses.

The effect of all this is that to successfuly call a touch of a method you need to navigate these course heads to get you back to rounds, while not repeating any row too many times and getting some nice musical rows. It is possible to end a composition at a set of rounds which isn't at a lead head (as we have shown), but this is less common. 

To keep track of where to shout calls, conductors can use calling positions. Each lead has a different calling position which describes where the conductors bell would be at the end of that lead should a call be made.

If, with a call, your bell ends up in the last place, it is known as a call at "home". If you end up at a place one from the back its known as wrong if you have an even number of bells (known as the stage) and middle if you have an odd stage. If you end up at a place two from the back it is the opposite, a middle for an even stage and a wrong for an odd stage. This definition isn't perfectly agreed upon and some publications may treat middle as always being two from the back and wrong as always being one from the back, so be careful. The reason for swapping these around is quite subtle and I'll explain it in more detail in the future.
// Caption: It is to ensure coursing order permutations are consistent across odd and even stages.

Next is before. If your bell ends up in 3rds place and the call does not have a bell making seconds place this is known as before. If a bell is making seconds place and you end up in 2nds place then it is also before. Thus, for common treble patterns this mean that before is when you are leading BEFORE the treble.
The rest of the calling positions are quite simple, in is if you end up in 2nds place, out is if you end up in 3rds place and the rest just correspond to their number (thirds, fourths, fifths and so on). These sorts of calls are less common as they effect the tenors on larger stages. When I refer to tenors I don't mean a bell covering, I am refuring to any bell with a number greater than 6. Don't worry about this for doubles methods (Comment: Doubles = A method with a stage of 5). It may be that multiple calling positions could be used for the same thing, for example, before out and thirds could all refer to the same lead.

Lets look at a simple composition for plain bob doubles. This is the form typically used to show compositions. Calling positions are shown at the top, bobs are shown with dashes and singles are shown with an s. A number before a dash or an s tells you how many times to repeat a call in that position. This simple composition is often abreviated IOM as we do one call at in, one call at out and one call at middle. This composition is often refered to as "In out make the bob" as a call at middle involves making the bob and this may be easier to remember.
Typically the observation bell is the tenor, so as we ring this method we would shout "Bob" when we would do 3/4 down as the bob makes us go "In". We then shout "Bob" when we would make seconds as the bob makes us go "Out". Finally we shout "Bob" when we would do 3/4 up as the bob makes us "make the bob" and end up in middle position.

If a composition (also known as a touch) is an extent it means that every differnet possible row has been rung once and only once. The number of possible rows can be calculated using the factorial function. Consider that to pick a random row we could pick from 5 numbers for the first place, 4 for the second place and so on, giving us 5x4x3x2x1 or 5 factorial differnet rows. A composition is true if no row is repeated.
// Caption: For a touch longer than an extent no row can be repeated more than one time more than another row.
The proof of truth for a composition is pretty complicated, so I won't cover it in this video.

Bobs typically swap around the work of three bells while singles typically swap around the work of only two bells. For methods on less than 6 bells this isn't quite true. For example, in plain bob doubles there is no possible single which only effects two bells. 
Moving up one bell, you can see that in plain bob minor the bob effects 3 bells and the single effects 2 bells. If we attempted this sort of single on plain bob doubles we would end up with the identity change where none of the bells move, and as this is not allowed to avoid repeated rows we have the single be this change. I haven't gone into detail about what you do at a single in plain bob doubles as it isn't used much, however the strategy to work out what you do at each lead is the same as for a bob.

There are rules used to work out the what you do at a bob or single in most methods. If you have a bell making a place over the treble and no other places at the lead end change you have a near call where the bells which are effected are the ones near the treble. If you have a place at the back at the lead end change you instead have a far call. These rules don't apply to all methods, and there are also other less common types of calls such as a big bob, but the two covered here are the most common.
