A call occurs whenever the conductor shouts "Bob" or "Single" in order to change what each bell does next. A call can happen at the end of any lead, and typically modifies the lead end change. For example, in plain bob doubles if a bob was called here then instead of doing this lead end change you would do this change. This then modifies the lead head of the next lead and so changes what the bells are doing next.

So lets look at what you have to do when there is a call in plain bob doubles and why. If you are 5ths place bell you are normally about to dodge 3/4 up and become 3rds place bell. If a bob is called you instead make a place at the lead end change. This makes you 4ths place bell, and 4ths place bell starts by hunting in and then doing 4 blows behind. So essentially this is your line, you make 4ths, hunt back in and then do 4 blows behind next time. This pattern is known as "making the bob". You have, in effect, started doing what the 2 would have done if there was no call. For the rest of the line where you would have seen the 2 you will instead see the 5.
If you are 3rds place bell instead of making seconds you hunt out and become thirds place bell, and so make seconds next time. If you are 2nds place bell instead of dodging 3/4 down you hunt in and become 2nds place bell, thus it is as if you are finishing making seconds and so do 3/4 down next time. Finally if you are 4ths place bell the call does not effect you, and so since your place bell has not changed you just do what you would have done normally.
All these changes can be visualised in our circle of work with this transition instead of the normal rotation. Notice how the bell which is doing 4 blows behind still does its normal rotation.

Before going further I want to get you comfortable with the concepts of permutations as they can be a big shortcut when dealing with calls. I mentioned in the previous episode that a single lead shuffles around the bells in a very specific way and that since the path the bells take in each lead is the same you can just apply this shuffle again to get to the next lead head. This set of lines describing how to move the bells around is known as a permutation. If you have a set of changes, you can ignore the rows in between the start and the end and just use a permutation to tell you what the result of those changes are for any initial row. You can also do this in reverse, working out what starting row to have to get the final row. If you know the permutation for a plain, bob and single lead you can easily check through a whole composition just by transposing the lead heads. This prevents you needing to write out all the rows inside the leads.

In this visualisation I am only showing the lead head of each lead in a composition. A white arrow represents a plain lead, and a blue arrow represents a lead where a bob is called near the end. To get from one lead head to the next I have used transpositions. This cycle shows the lead heads we cycle through with just plain leads. If we add a bob and continue having plain leads you can see that we have entered a new cycle and are unable to get back to rounds without a new call. This new cycle is known as a different course and each course can be thought of as the method but if it started with a different row instead of rounds. Each course has a course head, and for the normal course (known as the plain course) the "course head" is just rounds, however in this new course the course head is the lead head where our observation bell is in its natural position (where it was at the start). The observation bell is typically the bell that the conductor rings and its path is used to remember where to shout calls. It is typically the heaviest bell and so this case it is the 5. It doesn't matter which lead we start a course from, it is still the same course.

Looking closer at this diagram. First, remember that whenever we have a lead with a bob in it the bell in 4ths place is uneffected, but all the other bells swap around. These two rows represent the effect of a plain and a bob lead on this row. Note that the bell which is in fourths place (the 3) is in the same place for both as it is uneffected, so it would do the same work with our without a call. Looking at the next lead, notice how this shows us quite clearly which bells were effected by the call and how. The 4 has started doing what the 2 would have done, the 5 has started doing what the 4 would have done and the 2 has started doing what the 5 would have done, while the 3 remains uneffected. This row, which is the equivalent of rounds in the new course, can be very useful when creating compositions and is linked closely to coursing order. If we instead had a call such that the 5 was uneffected, we would end up with the 5 in its natural position at this row, and so the rounds equivalent would be our course head. To ensure that the 5 was uneffected I made sure to add a bob when it was in 4ths place. For compositions on more bells this is often the case, as the tenors are often left uneffected, and so the the rounds equivalent is always our coursehead and could be used for looking at how the bells swap with calls. The reason we have the course head defined as being the row with the observation bell in its natural position instead of just using the rounds equivalent is that it allows us to uniquely define the course head of each course, otherwise we could have two courses which are identical but defined by different course heads. For example this could allow us to define a course with this row as the course head, despite this course being identical to the plain course.

Now something to note is that the original course isn't the only one which contains rounds, and any row could be rounds if we pick the right course head. Note that to have a course head which we can actually get to using the normal calls we must pick a row with the treble leading. The effect of all this is that to successfuly call a touch of a method you need to navigate these course heads to get you back to rounds, while not repeating any row too many times and getting some nice musical rows. It is possible to end a composition at a set of rounds which isn't at a lead head (as we have shown), but this is less common. 

To keep track of where to shout calls, conductors can use calling positions. Each lead has a different calling position which describes where the conductors bell would be at the lead head of the next lead should a call be made. There can be multiple different names for the same calling position, for example, here out could also be called before. By default, calling positions refer to where the observation bell would end up with a bob. When thinking about singles the same name would refer to a different position depending on where the single takes the observation bell.

If, with a call, your bell ends up in the last place, it is known as a call at "home". If you end up at a place one from the back the name changes depending on if we have an even or odd number of bells. Since we have an odd number of bells it is known as middle, if we instead had an even number of bells it would be known as wrong. If you end up at a place two from the back it is the opposite, a middle for an even stage and a wrong for an odd stage. This definition isn't perfectly agreed upon and some publications may treat middle as always being two from the back and wrong as always being one from the back, so be careful. The reason for swapping these around is quite subtle and I'll explain it in more detail in the future.

Next is before. If your bell ends up in 3rds place and the call does not have a bell making seconds place this is known as before. We have this aswell as before as for larger stages these describe different positions. If a bell is making seconds place and you end up in 2nds place then it is also before. Thus, for common treble patterns this mean that before is when you are leading BEFORE the treble.
The rest of the calling positions are quite simple, in is if you end up in 2nds place and out is if you end up in 3rds place. Note that out does not have the same caviet as before. All other calling positions are just the number of the place where your bell ends up (thirds, fourths, fifths and so on). Other numbered calling positions on higher stages are less common as they can lead to calls which effect the heavier bells.

In compositions with more bells, the heavier bells are often uneffected by calls and thus if a row has the same back bells as the last non-bracketed row we omit them. The front six are never omitted which is why we didn't have any shorter rows before.
Ok back to the composition we had before. This composition is often refered to as "In, out, make the bob" as a call at middle involves making the bob and this can help you remember what work you do at each call as the conductor. Typically the observation bell is the tenor, so as we ring this method we would shout "Bob" when we would do 3/4 down as the bob makes us go "In". We then shout "Bob" when we would make seconds as the bob makes us go "Out". Finally we shout "Bob" when we would do 3/4 up as the bob makes us "make the bob" and end up in middle position. After all of this we end up with rounds.

An extent is defined as the complete set of possible rows for a given set of bells. For example, this is all the rows in an extent with five bells. The number of rows in an extent is the factorial of the number of bells. If you haven't seen the factorial function before, it is just the product of all numbers less than or equal to the input number. For example, 5 factorial is 5 times 4 times 3 times 2 times 1, giving us 120. To derive this formula, consider that to create a random row we have 5 options for the number we put in the first position, then 4 options for the second position and so on. A composition is true if it contains a number of extents (not neccessarily one after another) and any additional rows are distinct.
The proof of truth for a composition can get pretty complicated, but know that the in out make the bob composition was true as it contained a single extent and nothing more.

Bobs typically swap around the work of three bells while singles typically swap around the work of only two bells. Here I am showing the lead end changes for plain bob minor with and without calls. I have highlighted in blue the bells which are effected by the calls, and are thus in a different place to where they would be without a call. Notice how for a single, in order to effect only two bells we make the two bells which are dodging instead make a place. Let's try the exact same calls for plain bob doubles. This leads to the same number of bells being effected, but causes us an issue. This change is known as the identity change where all bells stay in the same place. This sort of change is not typically used as it leads to repeated rows. Unfortunately any other way to swap around two bells with a single requires a jump change, so a single which only effects two bells is not possible. We instead compromise and have a single which effects three bells. These sorts of reasons mean that the rule of a bob effecting three bells and a single effecting two bells don't always hold, especially when using a fewer bells. I haven't gone into detail about what you do at a single in plain bob doubles as it isn't used much, however the strategy to work out what you do at each lead given a call is the same as for a bob, and I'd highly recommend you work through it on a piece of a paper.

There are rules used to work out the what you do at a bob or single in most methods. If you have a bell making a place over the treble and no other places at the lead end change you have a near call where places made are near the treble. This is what we had earlier for plain bob. If you instead have a place at the back you have a far call. This is what the near and far calls look like. These rules don't apply to all methods, and there are also other less common types of calls such as a big bob, but the two covered here are the most common. Something I want to stress is that most compositions are on methods with at least 6 bells, which is why some rules are more complicated on lower numbers of bells, however I hope this video gave you a good understanding of the principles at play.
