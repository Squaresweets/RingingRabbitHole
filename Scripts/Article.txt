Visualising ringing concepts with animation
I have spent my university summer break writing and animating YouTube videos explaining topics from change ringing, both to make knowledge of these topics more accessable and to further my own understanding of them. One of the biggest problems I had when first learning ringing theory was finding resources. There are so many brilliant ringing resources online, but many are tucked away in archived PDFs or old websites. I decided to create an accessable resource visualising concepts which can be quite hard to grasp from books. A good example is how place bells work and how they interact with calls. Despite these concepts being thoroughly introduced to me, I didn't fully understand how they clicked together until I wrote them out and worked through them. Animating these videos allowed me to easily see how everything worked, and I hope that this also helps other people. So far, I have created four videos on method structure, calls, place notation, and coursing order.

I had planned to create the videos a while before I started animating them, and so had a backlog of scripts to animate when I got back from uni. All of the animations were coded in the programming language Python using a system called Manim, originally made for creating maths animations. I first made a system to render bluelines, then iterated from there, creating scripts to render circles of work and transpositions. One nice thing about this project was that the code could be very messy, as it only had to work once and spit out the animations. I started each video by iterating on the script, imagining the animations at each point, and running it past ringers of different skill levels to make sure it all made sense. Once I became more comfortable with how the videos were paced, many scripts had to be completely reworked to fit with the style. I animated paragraphs one by one, adapting the script as I went to better match the animations. The animation was by far the most rewarding part of this whole process and really gave me the opportunity to get ambitious with the visualisations. Once all paragraphs were complete, I recorded the voice-over using a microphone generously loaned by a local ringer and imported all of the videos into my video editing software. I then added freeze frames in between animations to line them up with my voice. All throughout this process, I have been very lucky to have the support of some very experienced ringers to answer my every question, and quite a few errors were spotted at this stage, which I could fix by redoing the animations. Once complete, I exported the videos and uploaded them to my YouTube channel. I decided on the series name "The Ringing Rabbit Hole" because of my many nights far too deep down that very rabbit hole, trying to understand niche ringing topics.

Throughout creation, I kept finding small areas which I didn't fully understand, things which I wouldn't have noticed if I wasn't animating them. A key example is everything that changes in a composition where the tenor is affected, as that information is glossed over in many resources. Each video took about two weeks to make, working in the evenings. Animations took up the vast majority of the time, with script writing, voice-over, and editing only taking about a day each.

I still have lots more ideas for videos on more complicated topics, such as false couse head groups and composing, and for simpler things, such as call changes for new ringers. So far, I am very pleased with the response to the videos I have put out. I am not planning to make any more videos this summer, and I am instead working on an update to my ringing app Campana, coinciding with some of the things I have mentioned in videos. I hope to work on more videos in the future because I've really enjoyed it!

If you have any questions regarding the creation of these videos, please do reach out, and I'd be happy to share code and ideas! I want to give a special thanks to Jadd Virji for the help throughout this process, and to everyone in the St Barnabas band and the CUGCR for the constant support.
